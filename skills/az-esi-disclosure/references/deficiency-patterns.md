# Deficient ESI disclosure: patterns

**Contents:** Identification P-1 to P-4 · Inquiry P-5, P-6 · Production P-7 to P-9 · Conference and scope P-10, P-11 · Privilege P-12, P-19 · Supplementing P-13 · Preservation and loss P-14 to P-16, P-18 · Consequences P-17

Each entry gives what the disclosure says, why it falls short, and what to ask for. Rule cites
point to `rules.md`, and case names point to `authorities.md`. Quote a rule or case only from
those files. These are starting points for the lawyer's judgment, not findings.

**Draft, 10/15/2026 (19 patterns). The firm should add the patterns it sees in practice.** Italic text in a heading is sample disclosure wording, not a quote from any source.

## Identification (Rule 26.1(a)(8)-(9))

**P-1. _All relevant ESI_, or _documents and ESI in Defendant's possession_.**
Why: 26.1(a)(9) requires the "existence, location, custodian, and general description" of the
ESI. A category label gives none of the four. By analogy, Solimeno (Link 6, an expert case) held that "extremely general" disclosures fell short.
Ask: each source by name (system, account, or device), where it sits, who controls it, and what
it holds, for each likely custodian.

**P-2. Email named, but no custodians.**
Why: "custodian" is one of the four required elements.
Ask: the custodians whose mail is being searched; whether shared or role mailboxes (HR@, info@)
are included; and the date range.

**P-3. Phones and texts missing where the facts point to them** (a supervisor's texts, a driver's
phone, a coworker group chat).
Why: 26.1(a)(9) reaches ESI that "may be relevant to the subject matter of the action", not just
the ESI the party plans to use; 26.1(e)(2) adds what reasonable inquiry would find.
Ask: each device and messaging app used by the key witnesses (texts, WhatsApp, Teams, Slack),
whether it is company-issued or personal, and whether it has been preserved.

**P-4. A business system missing where the claim runs through it** (timekeeping, payroll, HRIS,
dispatch or telematics, CRM, ticketing, door-access or camera logs).
Why: the same as P-3. These systems usually hold the most objective evidence in the case.
Ask: the system's name, what fields it keeps, how long it keeps them, who administers it, and an
export format that keeps the data.

## Inquiry (Rule 26.1(e)(2))

**P-5. _Plaintiff is not aware of any ESI_ or _none at this time_, from a party who plainly has it.**
Why: the duty covers what the party "can ascertain, learn, or acquire by reasonable inquiry and
investigation" (26.1(e)(2)). See Norwest and Scottsdale Princess (Link 2).
Ask: confirmation that the client was asked about each likely source, and a supplement.

**P-6. ESI held by an agent or vendor left out** (a payroll processor, an IT contractor, a cloud
provider, a former employee's account the company controls).
Why: possession, custody, or control (26.1(e)(2)) includes what the party can get from its agents.
Ask: the third-party sources and the party's access to each one.

## Production (Rule 26.1(c)(2)-(3))

**P-7. ESI identified but not produced within 40 days after the initial disclosure.**
Why: 26.1(c)(2) sets 40 days unless the parties agree or the court orders otherwise. Count under
Rule 6(a), and show the arithmetic.
Ask: production by a date certain, or the agreement or order that changed the deadline.

**P-8. Texts, chats, or spreadsheets produced as PDFs, printouts, or screenshots.**
Why: absent agreement, 26.1(c)(3) requires the form the receiving party requested, or else native
or another "reasonably usable form" with "the same ability to access, search, and display".
Images of messages lose dates, sender data, and thread context.
Ask: native files or a forensic export (with metadata) in the form requested, and a request in
writing if none was made.

**P-9. Metadata stripped, or a production that can't be searched.**
Why: the same as P-8.
Ask: load files with standard metadata fields, or native files; name the fields you need.

## Conference and scope (Rules 26.1(c)(1), 16, 26(b)(2), 26(e))

**P-10. No ESI conference held, or the other side sent no one who knows its systems.**
Why: 26.1(c)(1) requires the parties to confer promptly once ESI is disclosed, with "at least one
representative" who is "reasonably familiar" with the party's systems, and lists topics (A)-(H).
Ask: a conference date; a person who knows the systems; and an agenda covering (A)-(H).

**P-11. _Unduly burdensome_ or _not reasonably accessible_, with no support.**
Why: 26(b)(2)(B) places the burden on the party resisting; 26(e) requires an affidavit
describing the burden and estimating the expense, and lists the factors. 37(h) lets the court
allocate costs.
Ask: the affidavit; the specific sources claimed to be inaccessible; and a sampling or phased
proposal under 26.1(c)(1)(B)-(C).

## Privilege (Rules 26.1(h), 26(b)(6))

**P-12. ESI withheld as privileged, with no log.**
Why: 26.1(h)(1) requires prompt compliance with 26(b)(6)(A).
Ask: a log meeting 26(b)(6)(A), or the stipulated alternative format 26(b)(6)(A)(ii) allows. Also
ask about a Rule 502(d) order (26.1(c)(1)(H)).

**P-19. (Our side) The client's emails with its lawyers sit in a custodian's mailbox.**
Why: anything withheld for privilege must be logged under 26.1(h)(1) and 26(b)(6)(A), unless the
parties agree to an alternative under 26(b)(6)(A)(ii).
Ask: is any lawyer communication inside the date range being searched? If not, say so in the
disclosure. If so, propose a stipulation excluding post-complaint communications with litigation
counsel from the log. Tell the client not to gather or forward these emails.

## Supplementing (Rule 26.1(f)(2))

**P-13. New ESI turned up in a deposition or production but was never added by supplement.**
Why: supplements are due within 30 days of discovery (26.1(f)(2)). After the scheduling-order
deadline, or within 60 days of trial, use requires leave under 37(c)(4)-(5).
Ask: a supplement, or notice that you will oppose any later use.

## Preservation and loss (Rules 37(g), 45.2)

**P-14. No litigation hold, or a hold issued after the duty arose.**
Why: under 37(g)(1)(A)-(B) the duty begins when a party reasonably anticipates the action.
McMurtry and Ferneau (Link 7).
Ask: the date of the hold, who received it, which auto-delete settings were suspended, and when.

**P-15. ESI gone: a phone reset or replaced, an account closed, an auto-delete left running.**
Why: 37(g)(2) first asks whether the ESI can be restored or replaced through more discovery.
Ask: backups, carrier or cloud records, and the other side's copies. Imaging or forensic
restoration of the other side's devices needs a court finding under 26(b)(2)(B)(ii): relevance to
fraud or intentional misconduct, prejudice from a 37(g) preservation failure, or other good cause.
Then:
prejudice allows cure measures (37(g)(2)(A)); intent to deprive allows the presumption,
instruction, dismissal, or default (37(g)(2)(B)), and dismissal or default also needs prejudice.
Smyser warns that without bad faith an adverse inference is not required.

**P-16. Proving intent to deprive.**
Why: intent is rarely admitted. Jones (persuasive) looks to timing, affirmative deletion steps,
and selective preservation. Ferneau's eight factors govern whether dismissal fits.
Ask: a timeline of the deletions against the dates of notice. A forensic examination of the
other side's device needs the court finding in 26(b)(2)(B)(ii) (see P-15).

**P-18. Screenshots produced from a device that is later "replaced" or wiped.**
Why: the producing side chose what to capture, and the source is gone. That raises 37(g)(1)
(was the duty in place when the device went?), 26.1(c)(3) (form), and the Jones factors on
selective preservation (persuasive only).
Ask: the date the device was retired and who decided; whether it was imaged or backed up first
(cloud, carrier, or MDM records); who chose which messages to screenshot and how; and native
copies from the other participants' devices. Compare the replacement date with the preservation
letter and the date suit was anticipated.

## Consequences (Rule 37(c)-(d))

**P-17. Any of the above, once a deadline has passed.**
Why: 37(c)(1) excludes undisclosed information unless the court specifically finds no prejudice
or good cause; 37(c)(2) allows fees for inaccurate or incomplete disclosure; 37(d) allows serious
sanctions for knowingly withholding unfavorable information. Allstate and Gerow (common sense,
not a technical weapon) are the other side's best reply. Marquez and Wayne Cook decide whose
fault it is: the party's or the lawyer's.
Ask: in the letter, state the consequence and a cure date. In court, use the Rule 26(d) joint
statement (3 pages) after a good-faith consultation under 26(i) and 7.1(h).
